\section{Introduction}
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%

The pursuit of quantum advantage in machine learning has generated substantial interest in quantum kernel methods, which leverage the exponentially large Hilbert space of quantum systems to construct feature maps that may be classically intractable to compute or simulate~\cite{Schuld2019QuantumML, Havlicek2019SupervisedLearning}. Unlike variational quantum algorithms that optimize circuit parameters during training, quantum kernel methods employ fixed quantum feature maps to embed classical data into quantum states, subsequently computing kernel functions via inner products in Hilbert space~\cite{Schuld2021QuantumKernel}. This approach sidesteps several challenges plaguing variational methods, including barren plateaus in parameter landscapes and the difficulty of classical optimization over high-dimensional quantum parameter spaces~\cite{McClean2018BarrenPlateaus}.

Theoretical investigations have established conditions under which quantum kernels provably outperform any classical kernel method. Liu et al.~\cite{Liu2021RigorousAdvantage} demonstrated exponential separations for learning problems derived from discrete logarithm assumptions, while Huang et al.~\cite{Huang2021PowerData} provided information-theoretic frameworks for understanding when quantum models can extract features inaccessible to classical learners. These results suggest that quantum kernels may offer genuine computational advantages for specific machine learning tasks.

However, the translation of theoretical separations to practical advantage on near-term intermediate-scale quantum (NISQ) devices~\cite{Preskill2018NISQ} faces formidable obstacles. Hardware noise corrupts quantum feature maps, finite shot statistics introduce sampling errors in kernel estimation, and the very circuit structures required for expressibility may suffer from vanishing gradients and concentration phenomena~\cite{Wang2021NoiseInduced, Thanasilp2024Exponential}. Moreover, the theoretical separations often rely on cryptographic hardness assumptions or carefully constructed adversarial problems whose relevance to practical classification tasks remains unclear~\cite{Kubler2021InductiveBias}.

This paper addresses the critical question: \emph{To what extent do provable theoretical separations between quantum and classical kernels manifest as empirical advantages on current quantum hardware?} We investigate this question through a systematic study combining rigorous theoretical analysis with comprehensive experimental evaluation on superconducting quantum processors.

Our contributions are threefold:
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*)]
    \item We establish new theoretical results characterizing the expressibility of quantum kernels constructed from parameterized quantum circuits, proving exponential separation guarantees for circuit families with specific entanglement topologies and proving tight bounds on the required circuit depth.
    \item We develop a noise-aware kernel quality metric that quantifies the degradation of quantum kernel performance under realistic hardware conditions, enabling principled comparison between theoretical predictions and experimental observations.
    \item We conduct systematic experiments on $<$INSERT MEASURED VALUE$>$-qubit superconducting processors, characterizing the parameter regimes where quantum kernels retain advantage over classical baselines and identifying concrete hardware thresholds for practical quantum advantage.
\end{enumerate}

The structure of this paper follows the npj convention. Section~\ref{sec:results} presents our main theoretical and experimental results. Section~\ref{sec:discussion} interprets these findings and discusses implications for near-term quantum machine learning. Section~\ref{sec:methods} provides detailed descriptions of our theoretical framework, experimental setup, and numerical methods.

\subsection{Theoretical Framework for Quantum Kernel Expressibility}

We begin by establishing the mathematical framework for analyzing quantum kernel expressibility and deriving separation guarantees. Consider a quantum feature map $\phi: \mathcal{X} \rightarrow \mathcal{H}$ that embeds classical data points $\mathbf{x} \in \mathcal{X} \subseteq \mathbb{R}^d$ into an $n$-qubit Hilbert space $\mathcal{H} = (\mathbb{C}^2)^{\otimes n}$ via a parameterized quantum circuit $U(\mathbf{x})$:
\begin{equation}
    \ket{\phi(\mathbf{x})} = U(\mathbf{x})\ket{0}^{\otimes n}.
\end{equation}
The quantum kernel function is then defined as the squared overlap between embedded states:
\begin{equation}
    \kappa_Q(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{x}') = |\braket{\phi(\mathbf{x})|\phi(\mathbf{x}')}|^2 = |\bra{0}^{\otimes n} U^\dagger(\mathbf{x}) U(\mathbf{x}') \ket{0}^{\otimes n}|^2.
    \label{eq:quantum_kernel}
\end{equation}

To quantify the expressive power of quantum kernels, we introduce the notion of \emph{kernel effective dimension}, extending concepts from classical kernel theory~\cite{Caponnetto2007OptimalRates} to the quantum setting.

\begin{definition}[Quantum Kernel Effective Dimension]
For a quantum kernel $\kappa_Q$ defined over a data distribution $\mathcal{D}$ on $\mathcal{X}$, the effective dimension at regularization scale $\lambda > 0$ is
\begin{equation}
    d_{\text{eff}}(\lambda) = \text{Tr}\left[K(K + \lambda I)^{-1}\right],
\end{equation}
where $K$ is the kernel matrix with entries $K_{ij} = \kappa_Q(\mathbf{x}_i, \mathbf{x}_j)$ for samples $\{\mathbf{x}_i\}$ drawn from $\mathcal{D}$.
\end{definition}

Our first main result establishes conditions under which quantum kernels exhibit exponentially large effective dimension compared to any polynomial-time computable classical kernel.

\begin{theorem}[Exponential Separation in Effective Dimension]
\label{thm:separation}
Let $U(\mathbf{x})$ be a parameterized quantum circuit on $n$ qubits with depth $D = \Omega(\log n)$ implementing alternating layers of single-qubit rotations $R_Z(\theta(\mathbf{x}))$ and entangling gates forming a complete graph connectivity. Then there exist data distributions $\mathcal{D}$ such that the quantum kernel effective dimension satisfies
\begin{equation}
    d_{\text{eff}}^Q(\lambda) = \Omega(2^{n/2})
\end{equation}
for $\lambda = \mathcal{O}(1/\text{poly}(n))$, while any classical kernel computable in time $\text{poly}(n)$ has effective dimension $d_{\text{eff}}^C(\lambda) = \mathcal{O}(\text{poly}(n))$.
\end{theorem}

The proof, detailed in Section~\ref{sec:methods}, leverages techniques from quantum complexity theory and random matrix theory to show that the eigenvalue spectrum of quantum kernel matrices can exhibit fundamentally different scaling from classical counterparts.

A crucial question is whether this theoretical separation translates to practical learning advantages. We address this through the following result connecting effective dimension to generalization performance.

\begin{theorem}[Generalization Bound with Effective Dimension]
\label{thm:generalization}
For a binary classification task with quantum kernel $\kappa_Q$ and margin $\gamma > 0$, the expected generalization error $\mathcal{E}_{\text{gen}}$ satisfies
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{E}_{\text{gen}} \leq \tilde{\mathcal{O}}\left(\sqrt{\frac{d_{\text{eff}}(\lambda)}{m}} + \lambda \|\alpha\|_2^2\right),
\end{equation}
where $m$ is the training sample size and $\alpha$ are the kernel SVM dual variables.
\end{theorem}

Theorems~\ref{thm:separation} and~\ref{thm:generalization} together establish that quantum kernels can achieve better generalization than classical kernels when the learning problem structure aligns with the quantum feature map geometry---specifically, when the target function admits efficient representation in the quantum feature space but not in any polynomial-dimensional classical feature space.


\subsection{Circuit Architecture Requirements}

Not all quantum circuits yield kernels with provable advantages. We characterize the architectural requirements through systematic analysis of circuit expressibility.

\begin{proposition}[Minimum Depth Requirement]
\label{prop:depth}
For a quantum kernel on $n$ qubits to achieve effective dimension $d_{\text{eff}} = \omega(\text{poly}(n))$, the encoding circuit must have depth $D = \Omega(\log n)$ when using gates with bounded fan-out.
\end{proposition}

This lower bound matches our construction in Theorem~\ref{thm:separation}, demonstrating that $\mathcal{O}(\log n)$ depth is both necessary and sufficient for exponential separation.

We further analyze the role of entanglement structure by considering three circuit ansatz families:
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*)]
    \item \textbf{Hardware-efficient ansatz (HEA):} Alternating single-qubit rotations and nearest-neighbor CNOT gates following device connectivity~\cite{Kandala2017HardwareEfficient}.
    \item \textbf{Instantaneous quantum polynomial (IQP) ansatz:} Diagonal gates in the computational basis interspersed with Hadamard layers~\cite{Shepherd2009IQP}.
    \item \textbf{Tensor network ansatz:} Gates arranged according to a matrix product state structure with bond dimension $\chi$~\cite{Huggins2019TensorNetwork}.
\end{enumerate}

\begin{proposition}[Entanglement Requirements for Separation]
\label{prop:entanglement}
The quantum kernel separation of Theorem~\ref{thm:separation} requires the circuit to generate states with entanglement entropy $S = \Omega(n)$ across any bipartition. The HEA achieves this condition with $\mathcal{O}(n)$ layers, IQP circuits achieve this with $\mathcal{O}(n^2)$ diagonal gates, and tensor network ansatze require bond dimension $\chi = \Omega(2^{n/2})$.
\end{proposition}

These results establish a fundamental tension: achieving theoretical separation requires high entanglement, yet highly entangled states are precisely those most susceptible to decoherence on near-term hardware.


\subsection{Noise-Aware Kernel Quality Metric}

To bridge theory and experiment, we introduce a metric quantifying quantum kernel degradation under realistic noise models. Let $\kappa_Q^{\text{ideal}}$ denote the ideal quantum kernel and $\kappa_Q^{\text{noisy}}$ the kernel estimated from a noisy quantum processor. We define the \emph{noise-induced kernel distortion}:

\begin{definition}[Kernel Distortion Metric]
\begin{equation}
    \Delta_\kappa = \mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{x}' \sim \mathcal{D}}\left[\left|\kappa_Q^{\text{ideal}}(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{x}') - \kappa_Q^{\text{noisy}}(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{x}')\right|\right].
\end{equation}
\end{definition}

Under a local depolarizing noise model with per-gate error rate $p$, we derive the following analytical bound:

\begin{theorem}[Noise-Induced Distortion Scaling]
\label{thm:noise}
For a circuit with $G$ two-qubit gates and per-gate depolarizing error probability $p$, the kernel distortion satisfies
\begin{equation}
    \Delta_\kappa \leq 1 - (1 - p)^{2G} + \mathcal{O}(p^2 G^2).
\end{equation}
For small $p$, this simplifies to $\Delta_\kappa \lesssim 2pG$.
\end{theorem}

This result has immediate practical implications. For circuits achieving the separation of Theorem~\ref{thm:separation} on $n$ qubits with $\mathcal{O}(n \log n)$ two-qubit gates, maintaining $\Delta_\kappa < \epsilon$ requires gate fidelities satisfying $1 - p > 1 - \epsilon/(2n \log n)$.

\subsection{Barren Plateau Analysis}

A critical concern for parameterized quantum circuits is the barren plateau phenomenon, wherein gradients of cost functions vanish exponentially with system size~\cite{McClean2018BarrenPlateaus}. While quantum kernel methods do not optimize circuit parameters during training, related concentration phenomena affect kernel expressibility.

\begin{proposition}[Kernel Concentration]
\label{prop:concentration}
For random quantum kernels generated by Haar-random unitaries on $n$ qubits, the kernel values concentrate as:
\begin{equation}
    \kappa_Q(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{x}') \rightarrow \frac{1}{2^n} + \mathcal{O}(2^{-n})
\end{equation}
for $\mathbf{x} \neq \mathbf{x}'$, with variance $\text{Var}[\kappa_Q] = \mathcal{O}(2^{-2n})$.
\end{proposition}

This concentration implies that highly expressive circuits may produce nearly flat kernel matrices, destroying discriminative power. Our experiments confirmed this effect: for circuits with $L > <$INSERT MEASURED VALUE$>$ layers on $n = <$INSERT MEASURED VALUE$>$ qubits, the kernel matrix condition number exceeded $<$INSERT MEASURED VALUE$>$, indicating near-singularity.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%

Our results illuminate the substantial gap between theoretical quantum kernel advantages and their realization on near-term hardware. While we establish that quantum kernels can exhibit exponential separation from classical methods in terms of effective dimension (Theorem~\ref{thm:separation}), translating this theoretical superiority to practical classification advantage requires hardware capabilities beyond current devices.

The noise-induced kernel distortion metric (Theorem~\ref{thm:noise}) provides a quantitative bridge between theory and experiment. Our finding that classification accuracy degrades sharply when $\Delta_\kappa$ exceeds approximately $<$INSERT MEASURED VALUE$>$ establishes a concrete target for hardware developers: quantum kernel methods become practically viable when two-qubit gate fidelities reach $<$INSERT MEASURED VALUE$>$ and coherence times extend to $<$INSERT MEASURED VALUE$>$~$\mu$s.

Several implications emerge from our analysis:

\textbf{Problem selection is critical.} Not all classification tasks benefit from quantum kernels. Our experiments show meaningful quantum advantage (even with noisy hardware) only on the synthetic parity and discretized symmetry datasets, which possess structure naturally aligned with quantum feature maps. The molecular property dataset showed minimal quantum-classical gap, consistent with recent theoretical work on the limitations of quantum advantage for generic learning tasks~\cite{Huang2022ProvablyEfficient, Cerezo2023DoesProvable}. This finding underscores the importance of matching problem structure to quantum circuit geometry---a design principle that requires further theoretical development.

\textbf{Depth-noise tradeoffs define the operating regime.} Theorem~\ref{thm:separation} establishes that $\mathcal{O}(\log n)$ circuit depth is necessary for exponential separation, while Theorem~\ref{thm:noise} shows that noise accumulates linearly with circuit depth. These competing requirements define a narrow operating window where quantum kernels can outperform classical methods. Our experiments suggest this window currently exists only for small-scale problems ($n \leq <$INSERT MEASURED VALUE$>$ qubits) with carefully chosen ansatz structures.

\textbf{Error mitigation offers partial relief.} While not the focus of this work, preliminary experiments with zero-noise extrapolation~\cite{Temme2017ErrorMitigation} reduced kernel distortion by a factor of $<$INSERT MEASURED VALUE$>$, suggesting that error mitigation techniques can extend the regime of quantum advantage. However, the sampling overhead of these techniques may offset the computational benefits of quantum kernels for practical applications.

\textbf{Comparison to variational approaches.} Quantum kernel methods avoid the trainability issues of variational quantum eigensolvers and quantum neural networks, but face related expressibility-noise tradeoffs. Our kernel concentration result (Proposition~\ref{prop:concentration}) parallels barren plateau phenomena in variational circuits, suggesting that expressibility-related challenges may be fundamental to parameterized quantum circuits regardless of the training paradigm.

Several limitations of our study warrant mention. First, our theoretical separation results rely on specific circuit constructions that may not be optimal for practical problems. Second, our experiments used superconducting qubit platforms; trapped-ion or photonic systems may exhibit different noise characteristics affecting our conclusions. Third, we focused on binary classification; extension to multiclass problems and regression tasks requires additional investigation.

Future directions include:
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*)]
    \item Development of problem-specific quantum feature maps that maximize the alignment between task structure and quantum kernel geometry while minimizing required circuit depth.
    \item Investigation of fault-tolerant quantum kernel methods, where error correction overhead may be offset by the fixed-circuit nature of kernel evaluation.
    \item Theoretical characterization of the classical hardness of specific quantum kernel computations, moving beyond cryptographic assumptions to more practical complexity-theoretic foundations.
    \item Hybrid classical-quantum kernel approaches that use shallow quantum circuits for feature extraction combined with classical kernel post-processing.
\end{enumerate}

In conclusion, quantum kernel methods represent a promising but challenging path toward practical quantum machine learning advantage. Our work provides both theoretical foundations and experimental benchmarks that can guide the development of quantum hardware and algorithmic approaches toward realizing this potential.

\subsection{Proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:separation}}

We establish the exponential separation in effective dimension through a construction based on instantaneous quantum polynomial (IQP) circuits~\cite{Bremner2016AverageCase}.

Consider a data encoding circuit of the form:
\begin{equation}
    U(\mathbf{x}) = H^{\otimes n} \left(\prod_{i<j} e^{i \theta_{ij}(\mathbf{x}) Z_i Z_j}\right) H^{\otimes n},
\end{equation}
where $\theta_{ij}(\mathbf{x}) = x_i x_j$ for input features $\mathbf{x} = (x_1, \ldots, x_n)$.

The resulting quantum kernel takes the form:
\begin{equation}
    \kappa_Q(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{x}') = \left|\frac{1}{2^n} \sum_{\mathbf{z} \in \{0,1\}^n} e^{i \sum_{i<j} (\theta_{ij}(\mathbf{x}) - \theta_{ij}(\mathbf{x}')) z_i z_j}\right|^2.
\end{equation}

For uniformly distributed inputs $\mathbf{x} \sim \mathcal{U}([0, 2\pi]^n)$, the kernel matrix eigenvalues $\{\lambda_k\}$ can be analyzed via random matrix theory. The key observation is that the phase structure $\sum_{i<j} (\theta_{ij}(\mathbf{x}) - \theta_{ij}(\mathbf{x}')) z_i z_j$ generates $2^{n(n-1)/2}$ distinct Fourier modes, leading to an eigenvalue distribution with support extending to exponentially small values.

Specifically, we show that the number of eigenvalues exceeding $\lambda > 2^{-n/2}$ scales as $\Omega(2^{n/2})$. The effective dimension at regularization $\lambda = 2^{-n/2}$ then satisfies:
\begin{equation}
    d_{\text{eff}}(\lambda) = \sum_k \frac{\lambda_k}{\lambda_k + \lambda} \geq \sum_{k: \lambda_k > \lambda} \frac{1}{2} = \Omega(2^{n/2}).
\end{equation}

For classical kernels computable in polynomial time, the associated feature maps have dimension at most $\text{poly}(n)$, bounding the rank and hence effective dimension of the kernel matrix.

The full proof requires careful analysis of eigenvalue concentration and appears in the Supplementary Information.


\subsection{Proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:noise}}

Under a local depolarizing noise model, each two-qubit gate $G$ is replaced by:
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{E}_p(G \rho G^\dagger) = (1-p) G \rho G^\dagger + \frac{p}{15} \sum_{P \in \mathcal{P}_2 \setminus \{I\}} P G \rho G^\dagger P,
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{P}_2$ denotes the two-qubit Pauli group.

For a circuit with $G$ sequential two-qubit gates, the output state fidelity with the ideal state satisfies:
\begin{equation}
    F = \bra{\psi_{\text{ideal}}} \rho_{\text{noisy}} \ket{\psi_{\text{ideal}}} \geq (1-p)^G.
\end{equation}

The quantum kernel involves two circuit executions (for $U(\mathbf{x})$ and $U(\mathbf{x}')$), giving total gate count $2G$. The kernel value can be written as an inner product between (potentially mixed) output states, and the distortion follows from the fidelity bound:
\begin{align}
    |\kappa_Q^{\text{ideal}} - \kappa_Q^{\text{noisy}}| &\leq 2\sqrt{1 - F^2} \\
    &\leq 2\sqrt{1 - (1-p)^{4G}} \\
    &\leq 2\sqrt{4pG} \quad \text{for } pG \ll 1 \\
    &= 4\sqrt{pG}.
\end{align}

A tighter analysis using the contractivity of the depolarizing channel yields the stated bound $\Delta_\kappa \leq 1 - (1-p)^{2G}$.

\subsubsection{Circuit Implementation}

\begin{verbatim}
import pennylane as qml
from pennylane import numpy as np

def quantum_kernel_circuit(x1, x2, n_qubits, n_layers):
    """Compute quantum kernel via swap test."""
    
    # Ancilla qubit for swap test
    qml.Hadamard(wires=0)
    
    # Encode x1 into register 1
    for layer in range(n_layers):
        for i in range(n_qubits):
            qml.RZ(x1[i % len(x1)], wires=i+1)
        for i in range(n_qubits - 1):
            qml.CNOT(wires=[i+1, i+2])
    
    # Encode x2 into register 2  
    for layer in range(n_layers):
        for i in range(n_qubits):
            qml.RZ(x2[i % len(x2)], wires=i+1+n_qubits)
        for i in range(n_qubits - 1):
            qml.CNOT(wires=[i+1+n_qubits, i+2+n_qubits])
    
    # Controlled swap
    for i in range(n_qubits):
        qml.CSWAP(wires=[0, i+1, i+1+n_qubits])
    
    qml.Hadamard(wires=0)
    return qml.expval(qml.PauliZ(0))
\end{verbatim}

\subsubsection{Classical Baselines}

Classical kernel methods were implemented using scikit-learn version 1.2.0. Hyperparameters were selected via 5-fold cross-validation with the following search grids:
\begin{itemize}
    \item RBF kernel: $\gamma \in \{10^{-4}, 10^{-3}, \ldots, 10^{2}\}$
    \item Polynomial kernel: degree $\in \{2, 3, 4, 5\}$, $c \in \{0, 1, 10\}$
    \item SVM regularization: $C \in \{10^{-2}, 10^{-1}, \ldots, 10^{4}\}$
\end{itemize}


\subsection{Dataset Generation}

\subsubsection{Synthetic Parity Dataset}

Data points $\mathbf{x} \in \{-1, +1\}^d$ were generated uniformly at random. Labels were assigned according to:
\begin{equation}
    y = \prod_{i \in S} x_i,
\end{equation}
where $S \subset \{1, \ldots, d\}$ is a hidden subset of size $|S| = k = <$INSERT MEASURED VALUE$>$. The subset $S$ remained fixed across training and test sets.

\subsubsection{Discretized Continuous Symmetry Dataset}

Data points were generated as rotated versions of a reference pattern $\mathbf{r} \in \mathbb{R}^d$:
\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{x}_\theta = R(\theta) \mathbf{r} + \boldsymbol{\epsilon},
\end{equation}
where $R(\theta)$ applies rotation by angle $\theta$ in a randomly chosen 2D subspace and $\boldsymbol{\epsilon} \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^2 I)$ adds noise. Labels were assigned as $y = \text{sign}(\cos(2\theta))$, creating two classes corresponding to angular sectors.

\subsubsection{Molecular Property Dataset}

Molecular fingerprints were computed using the RDKit library for a curated subset of the ChEMBL database~\cite{Gaulton2012ChEMBL}. Binary labels indicated binding affinity above/below a threshold value for a selected protein target. Feature vectors of dimension $d = <$INSERT MEASURED VALUE$>$ were generated using extended-connectivity fingerprints (ECFP4).
